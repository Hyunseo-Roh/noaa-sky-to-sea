Ocean and atmosphere share one name: the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. At the surface, where the two meet, NOAA keeps instruments in the water and people on it.

Southeast of Nantucket

A buoy that reports to everyone

Southeast of Nantucket, NOAA weather buoy 44008 rides the open Atlantic. Like the other buoys of NOAA's National Data Buoy Center, it reports wind, waves and water temperature.

The readings go online, where anyone can read them.

Baltimore, Maryland

The water level, every six minutes

On a pier in Baltimore, Maryland, a NOAA tide station measures the water level every six minutes. It is part of NOAA's National Water Level Observation Network, which has more than 200 long-term stations.

Hudson River, New York

Charts start with sound

Before a nautical chart is drawn, the seafloor is surveyed. NOAA's Office of Coast Survey uses hydrographic data, collected with sonar, to make the charts that ships navigate by.

Here, NOAA Ship Thomas Jefferson brings its side scan sonar back aboard after a survey of the Hudson River.

Carysfort Reef, Florida Keys

Back to the reef

Carysfort Lighthouse stands over Carysfort Reef, in the Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary. Below the surface, nursery-grown corals go back onto Florida Keys reefs piece by piece, fixed in place with marine epoxy.

NOAA and its partners grow and plant corals to help restore Florida's reefs. In 2018, divers replanted staghorn coral here.
